THE FUNCTION THAT KNEW

To decide whether a unit can see its target, the game walks the line and asks every wall whether it straddles it. It has always done this. Here is what it did with the answer:

// Line of sight: blocked if any wall obstacle straddles the segment a→b.
function hasLOS(ax, az, bx, bz) {
  /* …walk the segment in 4u steps… */
      if (ox * ox + oz * oz < o.r * o.r) return false;   ← it is holding `o`
}

It finds the exact piece of wall that blocks the shot, and returns false. The identity of the obstruction — the one thing a sieger needs in order to do something about it — is discarded on the way out of the function.

So every caller that needed it went looking again, and what they all reached for was a guess: the nearest live wall in the target's camp. Which is a different question with a different answer.

DRAG THE TANK
Both rules pick a wall to shell. Move around the fort and watch them disagree. The circle is the Lurcher's 42u gun.

The nearest wall depends on where you happen to be standing relative to the ring. The blocking wall is on the line by construction — so if the target is inside your reach, the blocker is too, and breaking it makes the line. That is the entire siege-flatten rule, and it needs no second algorithm to state it.

function losBlocker(ax, az, bx, bz, skipTeam) {   // returns the obstacle, or null
      if (ox * ox + oz * oz < o.r * o.r) return o;
}
function hasLOS(ax, az, bx, bz) { return !losBlocker(ax, az, bx, bz); }

One rule replaced three: the latched breach wall, the nearest-wall scan, and a special case for the keep. That last one is worth a sentence, because it stopped being special. A sight-line to the keep's centre dies inside the building's own obstacle circle, so line-of-sight can never come true against it and a sieger would park and stare — but that was never an exception. It is the general rule working: with every wall down, the first thing on the line to the keep is the keep. Square onto it and shell it open.

THE VETO THAT COST A THOUSAND SECONDS

That fixed the units that arrived somewhere useful. It did nothing for the ones that were never given anywhere to go.

Choosing a firing position is a ladder: try the comfortable spot, then a less comfortable one, then — added earlier this week — a spot with no sight-line at all, on the reasoning that a unit can make its own line by demolishing what's in front of it. Each rung gives up something. But one test was never on the ladder at all. Crossfire — standing where a second tower can also reach you — was an absolute veto, on every rung.

As a default that is exactly right; it is what a standoff is for. Held as a veto, it answers "is this comfortable" when the question being asked is "can I shoot from anywhere at all" — and it would rather return nothing than an ugly yes.

THE RELAXATION LADDER
The shape of seed 25 — the target gun sits on the compound wall, so half its firing band is inside the enemy ring where nothing can drive, and a second tower covers the open ground. Step down the rungs; green cells are positions the solver would accept.

On seed 25 that produced 508 candidate cells and zero answers, twenty-two times over. A Lurcher sat for a thousand seconds while its commander re-picked siege, forever, and the match was logged as a stalemate with both keeps untouched.

A Lurcher kills a tower in 3.2 seconds. Standing under two guns for 3.2 seconds to remove one of them is a trade a person makes without thinking about it. Sitting still for a thousand seconds is not a trade at all.

So crossfire became the last rung instead of a veto, and nothing above it changed — a spot out of the crossfire always wins, including one with no sight-line, because chewing through a wall is slow but survivable and two arcs are neither. The rung only ever runs where the answer used to be nothing.

THE LANE NOBODY WAS CHARGED FOR

Meanwhile, watching a live match: a Valkyrie cracks a keep, then the commander scores a back-door capture at +15.7 — with two live towers sitting on the back door. The breakdown did not mention them. Not weighted lightly. Absent.

capture-rear +15.7 (flag OPEN +4, spare FB +1.5, fleet F +0.6, rogue +3.2, rogue +6.4)
siege-back   +11.9 (rear towers standing +3, opens rear capture +1, …)

The capture term counted towers that were dead on that side and paid a bonus for each. Live guns scored nothing — not a penalty, just an absent bonus. The same two towers raised the siege plan and were invisible to the capture plan.

It was also blind to the enemy FOB, whose guns sit nowhere near the keep, belong to no side of it, and shoot runners all the same. And it assumed a map has a tidy front and rear, which a designed map need not.

The replacement asks the only question that survives all three problems: walk the waypoints the runner will actually drive, and count the guns we know about that cover them.

THE MISTAKE INSIDE THE FIX

The first weighting charged a flat penalty per gun. It measured worse: average match time up about 10% on both seed sets with resolution unchanged, and the four directions landing within a point of each other. Both symptoms had one cause, and the button above is it.

Every route into a base converges on the keep, so the keep's own guns cover the final run-in whichever way you come. That common floor is the price of capturing at all. Billing it to each direction made capture globally timid and drowned the differences between lanes in a shared constant.

a flat charge for going in at all, while guns are up
+ the excess over the safest lane — the part that actually picks a direction

Which is what the toggle shows: four lanes at −11.8/−11.8/−16.8/−11.8 become −3.1/−3.5/−10.1/−4.5, and the lane that gets punished is the one that drives past the FOB.

A NUMBER THAT LIED

One counter went the wrong way all day. Relaxing the crossfire veto took the default set's empty solves from 69 to 4 — and the fresh set's from 202 to 265. A relaxed test producing more failures should be impossible.

It was a per-call counter. One hopeless target asked a hundred times read exactly like a hundred hopeless targets. Measuring the reject reasons directly: four of five fresh seeds produced no failures at all, and the entire count came from one target, on one seed, asked 98 times.

The loop behind it is a small comedy. The solver caches its null so it won't re-search a hopeless tower — and the very next line advanced the plan's cursor, which ran it past the end of the kill order, which rebuilt the plan, which allocated a fresh cache and threw away the null written one line earlier. The other code path that does the same job had already learned this and says so in its own comment. The two had disagreed ever since.

Both now leave the cursor alone, and the counter counts distinct targets. Removing the loop changed no outcomes — the default set's results are identical seed for seed — which is the honest result: it was a hundred pointless full-grid searches against a target that was unreachable either way. What it buys is the work not done, and an alarm you can believe.

A number that rises when the thing it names gets better is worse than no number at all. This one nearly sent a day's work chasing a phantom.

WHAT IT COST

The crossfire trade is not free, and the average hides it. Between the fix before it and the fix after, the default set's average match went 357s → 425s and swap loops rose from 34 to 38. Seed 116 went from 313 seconds to 973.

That is what taking fire from two towers instead of standing idle looks like. Units drive into positions they can be hurt in, and sometimes they lose the trade and have to be replaced. Zero stalemates bought at the price of longer grinds — which is a judgement about how the game should feel, not a bug, and the next thing to sit and watch rather than measure.

WHAT'S STILL WRONG

The Jotun never arrives. On two of three seeds checked it reaches a firing position on zero decisions, despite being handed a good one every time — median 45–79u against an 80u gun, never once out of range, the solver never coming back empty. The gun is fine and the spot is fine. Something in the commute isn't.

Unreachable-goal violations, ~61 a run on the default set, unmoved by anything today.

The default set is the hard set. Dry trips run about forty times higher there than on the fresh seeds and nav alarms three to four times higher, stably, across every change. Different island layouts. It is a standing reminder that a one-set result is not a verdict — which this project has now learned twice.

Also on the record: mid-session I reported that the Jotun spends its life on intercept duty. It doesn't. The probe kept sampling after the match ended, when the clock is frozen and every unit holds its last pose, so six thousand identical ticks piled onto whatever the field was doing in the final second. Its real mission mix is siege and defend. The harness has a comment warning about exactly this, which I had read.
