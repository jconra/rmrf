№23 — THE MAP IT NEVER LOOKED AT

2026-08-06 · six identical deaths, a lab that already had the answer, and three fixes that didn't earn their keep

Seed 1053. A Lurcher rolls out of the base, drives a hundred and eighty units to the enemy fort, arrives, and dies fifty-eight seconds later. The next Lurcher rolls out, drives the same route, and dies fifty-eight seconds later. This happens six times.

Look at the hull column. 220→220. Not one of them takes a scratch. They are not dying in a fight — they are being destroyed by the stuck-watchdog for standing still. And they are standing still at their assigned firing position, with a clean line of sight and a full magazine of sixty-eight rounds, having never pulled the trigger once.

A FIRING POSITION BELONGS TO A GUN

The firing position was 54 units from the tower. A Lurcher's gun reaches 42.

The siege plan solves a standoff once per tower and caches it, which is what makes the whole relaxation ladder affordable. But the ring it searches is sized from the current chassis — reach and hold range — while the cache is keyed by the wall alone. Red had flown a Valkyrie earlier in that siege. A Valkyrie reaches 80. It solved a perfectly good spot for itself, parked it in the cache, and every Lurcher that followed inherited a firing position it could not physically shoot from.

Each cached spot now records the gun it was drawn for, and re-solves when the current gun is shorter. A longer gun inheriting a close spot still reaches, so that direction is left alone and the expensive search stays rare. Seed 1053 went from the twenty-minute cap to resolving.

THE OBSERVATION THAT MATTERED

There is a standoff playground in lab/ — a little 2D sandbox for exactly this problem, and it works. It reliably finds a good spot. So the question, reasonably enough, was why the game finds it so hard.

The answer is that they were not doing the same thing at all.

the labthe game
reachabilityone breadth-first flood over the whole grid none
candidates consideredevery reachable cell — ~8100 24 points on each of 3–4 fixed radii
costone passup to five full-grid A* searches, then give up

The game leaned on a cheap filter called drivableTo, which compares landmass ids. That answers "is this the same island" — not "can this hull get there". It knows nothing about walls, gates, or a channel three cells wide. So the real question had to be asked with A*, A* is expensive, and the search was rationed to about five probes. A tower whose five probes all landed badly was simply abandoned.

The lab was not smarter. It just had a map.

SO THE GAME GOT ONE

reachFrom(v) floods outward from the unit's own cell using the same blocked test A* uses, eight-connected, across about nine thousand cells, cached for a beat so a siege solving several towers floods once. Every cell it touches is somewhere that hull can actually drive.

The standoff search now scans the entire annulus around the tower and takes the nearest cell that passes range, line of sight, and crossfire. One flood replaces five A* searches: cheaper and exhaustive. The search stopped being rationed, and towers stopped being abandoned for want of probe budget.

One detail that had to be right: the flood originally squeezed through diagonal gaps that A* refuses — the router will not clip a wall corner. A field that promises ground the router won't deliver is worse than no field, because the unit sets out, gets no route, and stands there until the watchdog kills it. The flood now uses the router's corner rule.

THE OTHER WALL: WINNING ON PAPER

With units suddenly closing to twelve units instead of parking at fifty-four, more matches reached a state the AI could not finish: enemy keep cracked, enemy fleet gone, and a stalemate anyway.

Seed 116 is the clean case. Blue is annihilated — zero units, zero reserves, keep at 0hp, all four towers down. Red spends the last six minutes running capture-front 7.5 in a Lurcher, which cannot carry a flag. It holds no Firebrat and no scrap to build one, and there are parts lying on the field the whole time. Scavenge — go get the parts, build a runner, walk in — scored 4.

The scorer already knew. Capture carried a term reading no runner −6. But −6 prices an impossible plan as merely a weak one, and capture still won the vote. A capture with nothing that can carry a flag cannot produce a win no matter how good the rest of the board looks, so it is now priced out of contention entirely, and fetching the parts that restore a runner is worth +10 when their base is already open. Same genuine binary as "there is an enemy" — a fact, not a spectrum.

THREE FIXES THAT DIDN'T EARN THEIR KEEP

All three were plausible, all three were built, all three were measured, and none of them shipped. They are worth more written down than deleted.

Tower fire releases the keep — a sieger locked on a walled HQ it can't shoot should switch to the gun grinding it down default-set transit-stuck 1585 → 625 fresh-set scuttles 1 → 18, alarms 7 → 28
Never swap vehicles mid-intercept — the plan expires before the swap completes no-op swaps 54 → 5 and 82 → 2 three stalemates, default alarms 10 → 31
Re-validate a cached firing spot the current unit can't reach nothing measurable 30/30 → 29/30, alarms 10 → 30, stuck went up

The pattern took three failures to see, and it is the most useful thing to come out of the night:

Every change that added a decision cost churn — stalemates, alarms, scuttles.
The change that replaced a bad search with a good one won outright.

A new decision point is a new place for the plan to change its mind, and this AI has spent months being taught to stop changing its mind. Fixing what a search knows does not add one.

WHAT'S STILL WRONG

Two counters got worse and both are honest signals rather than noise.

No-op swaps, up 31%. A commander pulls a unit off the field to trade it for something better, and the lift brings back the same chassis. The cause is known: the recall is placed on a plan that expires in the one second between the unit despawning and the next one deploying — overwhelmingly an intercept, the most perishable plan there is. Each instance wastes about a second. Blocking them measured worse, so the behaviour is tolerated and the alarm was re-tuned to count a run rather than a tally: the 285-in-a-row pathology still screams, the tolerated couple no longer does.

Dry trips, ~2.5 a match. A unit drives all the way to the enemy base and never fires a shot. This alarm was built during this same investigation, specifically because that failure is invisible to everything else — the unit isn't stuck, isn't lost, and its commander is picking a sensible mission the whole time. It just never accomplishes anything. It is now the loudest thing left, and it is next.
