№22 — THE DANCE

2026-07-28 · five wrong theories, one screenshot, and the bug that was two bugs

Two Lurchers meet in the middle of the map. They do not fight, exactly. They hold their distance, mirror each other's movements, drift across the island together, and occasionally trade a shot that kills nobody. It looks — the word came up every time — like a dance.

It took five days and five wrong answers to work out why. This is the write-up, including the wrong answers, because the wrong answers are most of the story.

THE THEORIES THAT DIED

The first four went the same way. Form a hypothesis, build a probe, watch the probe disagree.

theoryhow it died
The duel fire-gate keys off hull instead of turret, so they can't shoot Measured 1.77 shots per pair-second, with kills. They shoot fine.
The attack mission is holding an empty point Probe showed the "enemy position" was the unit's own position, flagged heard. Built the fix, reverted it.
The standoff spot is being placed in water Legal shallows. isLand:false but blocked:false — and firing from the shallows is intended.
The _standRot bearing walk is rotating them 8 rotations against 382 goal jumps. Not the cause; deleted it anyway.

The fifth was the interesting failure. A headless probe DID reproduce a freeze — two units locked, not shooting, holding distance. The cause was real: gun-report pings never decayed in the sim, so units "heard" gunfire from minutes earlier and froze on ghosts. It was a genuine bug and it meant every AI-vs-AI tournament ever run had broken hearing.

It was also not the dance. It could not be — the decay ran correctly in the render loop, so it could never happen in a game anyone was watching. Fixed it, banked it, started over.

The lesson that kept repeating: a reproduction is not a diagnosis. Four hypotheses died on contact with a probe, and the one that survived contact turned out to be a different bug wearing the same symptoms.

THE SCREENSHOT

What broke it open was not a probe. It was a paused game and a console dump.

CYAN  firedRung=underAttack   fof=-1.8   mnv=KITE → (-40,140)   drv=rev 1.0
RED   firedRung=underAttack   fof=+0.4   mnv=KITE → (40,-160)   drv=fwd 1.0
dist=42                                  both hitByVeh=recent

Three numbers, and the whole thing falls out.

42. The gap between them was 42.05 units. A Lurcher's SHOT_REACH is 42 — the range at which its slug physically expires. They were balanced precisely on the edge of their own weapon, which is why they traded the odd hit and never landed enough to finish.

Both fleeing. Both maneuver orders read KITE, and both were driving away — to points 70 and 215 units off, in opposite directions.

−1.8 and +0.4. Their fight-or-flight scores. One had judged the fight lost. The other had judged it winnable. They did the same thing anyway.

NEITHER ALLOWED TO LEAVE, NOR TO FIGHT

The priority ladder puts underAttack above engaging. Its job is "an inescapable rival is on top of us — answer it," and it decides that by asking whether we can outrun the enemy:

if (v.flyer || (SPEED[s.type] || 2) > (SPEED[et] || 2)) return false;   // can outrun it
if (v.underFire) return true;

In a mirror match that test can never pass. A Lurcher can never outrun a Lurcher. So underAttack fired for both, and because it sits above engaging, it forced engage without ever consulting the fight score. The unit at −1.8 was pinned in a fight it had already decided to leave.

Then, inside engage, the kite branch retreated — including for the unit at +0.4, which had correctly judged its rival weaker. That branch never looked at the score either.

So each unit was held in a state it could not act on. underAttack blocked disengaging; kite blocked committing. What we had been calling a dance was two units simultaneously trying to leave a fight neither was permitted to leave.

AND KITE WAS WALKING THEM OFF THE MAP

Kite falls back toward view.support — friendly tower cover — which resolves to the home base. For RED that was 215 units away. "Retreat to cover" was ordering two duelling units to march to opposite corners of the island while facing each other.

That is the travelling. In the baseline, 1810 of 2145 kite orders were aimed more than 60 units away — 84%. Not an edge case. The normal case.

One more thing fell out of the same branch. On an omni chassis — the Lurcher's six legs — KITE renders as a full-speed backpedal; the driver's own comment says so. Which means the separate report from a week earlier, "the Lurcher walks straight backwards at a Jotun and dies," was the same bug. One branch, two symptoms, filed as two tickets.

THE FIX, AND WHAT IT COST

Three changes, all in the same place: underAttack stops forcing engage below cfg.fofBail; kite consults the fight score before retreating and its 0.55 hull cliff becomes a gradual urge with hysteresis; and the fallback is capped at 34 units — a reposition, not an evacuation.

Units that used to be pinned now disengage — and some of them get stuck doing it near water. That is a real regression and it is on the list.

THE SECOND STORM

40 pathfinding searches a frame, and the arithmetic that caught it

Same night, different symptom: 60 replans a second, 16fps, and this on the perf panel —

fps 16   work 61.5ms      units 2
ai        58.1ms          ← 94% of the frame, for TWO units
render     3.2ms
replans/s 40
replan cause
  goal_moved/suppress   3  ← only THREE tagged

40 replans, 3 accounted for. Those two counters are incremented in different places — the cause tag inside the nav layer, the rate counter inside the pathfinder itself. The gap meant something was calling A* that wasn't the nav layer.

It was the siege standoff solver, run through an escape hatch. When a unit sits outside its own weapon reach for 12 seconds without closing, it re-solves its firing position — up to 40 full-grid searches, called directly, outside the frame budget.

And it looped, for a reason worth writing down. The solver ranks candidate spots by distance from the unit, and the unit is moving — so every re-solve returns a slightly different spot. The driver treats a GOTO more than 2 units from the standing one as a brand-new order, which resets the once-per-order guard that was supposed to stop the re-solving. Each solve therefore licensed the next one. Both escape hatches carried a comment saying "re-solve it once". Neither counted anything.

THE ASYMMETRY UNDERNEATH

Why did the driver keep rejecting spots the solver had just verified? Because they applied different standards, and the driver's was right:

if (path.budgetHit) return;   // a truncated search proves NOTHING about reachability

A* has a node cap. Hit it, and you get a partial route flagged budgetHit, meaning "ran out of budget, the goal might be fine, just far". Empty the open set instead and the answer is real. The driver checks that flag; its comment records that ignoring it once cost 4 points of resolution. The solver never checked it at all — so it accepted spots off truncated searches that the driver then correctly refused, and the refusal re-ran the solver.

The fix follows the standing instruction: one good search beats hundreds of poor ones. Candidate count dropped from 40 to 5, each with 2.5× the node budget, and budgetHit is now honoured with a third answer — unsure — so far-but-unproven is no longer confused with unreachable. Re-solves are counted per tower; the second failure moves down the kill order instead of trying again forever.

Standoff searches now run at 17 out of 4923 total A* calls — 0.3%. And every call is attributed by caller, so the arithmetic that caught this is now just a line on the panel.

BLAME THE CHASSIS, NOT THE PLAN

a new weight, from watching a Lurcher scuttle for 80 minutes

Observed in a 4907-second match: a Lurcher takes the siege mission, dies with nothing to show, gets replaced by another Lurcher, dies, and repeats. Eventually the commander fields a Jotun and it clears the towers in one tour.

The failure memory keyed on the mission alone. So a plan that was correct — siege those towers — but attempted with the wrong tool got benched wholesale. The commander threw away a good plan when it should have changed vehicle.

Failures are now recorded against the pairing: siege|lurcher, not siege. When a mission carries a negative score AND one chassis is the one failing it, the commander fields a different one that hasn't:

GREY: 1 lurcher lost on this siege plan with nothing to show — the plan's fine, the vehicle isn't. Sending a jotun.

One subtlety cost an hour. Failures are recorded against the vehicle that actually died, which is the type after roster substitution — but the check was asking about the raw role want. If the role table wanted a Jotun and the roster forced a Lurcher, the counts could never match the query, and the swap fired exactly zero times. Ask about the vehicle you are actually going to field.

ALARMS, ON PURPOSE

every bug this week was found by something repeating

Worth noticing how each of these was actually caught. The write-off storm: a log line repeating 80 times in two seconds. The re-solve storm: two counters disagreeing. The dance: a console dump from a paused game. None of them were found by reasoning about the code. They were found because something got loud.

So that is now deliberate rather than lucky. Four new alarm classes, each printing plain language with the numbers, and each counted in the tournament summary so a regression turns up in the gate instead of in a playtest:

  • [A* ALARM] — more than 12 pathfinding searches in one frame, naming the callers. This one would have caught the re-solve storm on the first frame it happened.
  • [SIEGE ALARM] — firing positions re-solved 6+ times in a match: the plan is thrashing, not adapting.
  • [LOSS ALARM] — 4 units lost in a row on one mission. The 80-minute Lurcher would have announced itself four deaths in.
  • Vehicle swaps, logged as the good news they are.

The principle, stated plainly: a loud alarm you can act on beats a rare bug you have to reconstruct. Five dead theories is what the alternative costs.

AND THE SEA

because not everything is a decision loop

The surf got a lab. Every shoreline constant — band width, wave roll-in, crest shape, froth, and the water's surface ripple — is now a live uniform driven from /lab/surf.html, which renders the real island through the real terrain material. Whatever gets dialled in there is what the game does; there is no porting step and no lab-versus-game drift.

Three things it fixed. The foam band was measured in height, so its width on the ground scaled inversely with beach slope and flat shelves — inland seas especially — whited out completely; it can now be normalised by slope and capped so the wash stays off the grass. The crests all broke in unison because the along-coast phase spread was 3.0 against a full cycle of 6.28. And the sea looked linear and repetitive because the chop was summed cosines, which are periodic and axis-aligned by construction — the mid and fine chop are now sampled from noise and differenced for a slope, with large slow wind patches leaving some water glassy and ruffling the rest.

One trap worth recording: the first version used fwidth() to measure beach slope. Derivatives are core in GLSL ES 3.00 but an extension in 1.00, and three.js only emits the pragma on WebGL1 — so it compiled perfectly under the headless SwiftShader rig and failed on a real GPU. The slope now comes from the terrain's vertex normal, which carries the same information and needs no extension anywhere. A test rig that can't fail the way production fails is not testing production.

THE LEDGER

what shipped, what's measured, what's still open

SHIPPED

  • Shot-awareness — a hit from outside the sight cone now records where it came from. Previously a turret hit stored a position and a vehicle hit stored only a timestamp, and underFire was only ever read after a sighting had already passed. 73% / 15% / 44% of under-fire ticks across three seeds came from an attacker the unit could not see.
  • noReach — a contact the driver cannot route to is remembered as such. Clearing the sighting alone was futile: sight rewrote it the next tick, and the unit spent every tick writing off the same contact. 90 write-offs per match → 1.
  • Fight-or-flight: the dance fix above.
  • Surf, the lab, and the water look.
  • The AI Lab's doctrine layer now charts MissionScore — it had been drawing the cascade that MissionScore replaced back in July, with seven of its ten rungs unreachable.

STILL OPEN

  • Endgame conversion. Every stalemate in every run this week is the same shape: a winner with the enemy flattened and no runner left to capture with.
  • Flee-stuck. The cost of the dance fix — units now disengage, and some wedge near water while doing it.
  • Deploy-then-swap churn. A unit rolled out, then recalled four seconds later because the mission re-scored.
  • The elevator shield. A unit tops out with five seconds of total invulnerability and immediately drives out of it, because mustGo outranks every combat rung. Designed, not yet built.
